Health Logbooks: Keeping Track of Medicines, Blood Pressure, and Blood Sugar

When a doctor asks "How has your blood pressure been?" or "Did you miss any doses?", a written record is far more reliable than memory. A paper health logbook is cheap, needs no batteries or apps, and can be handed across the desk at an appointment.

What to track

Follow your doctor's advice, but common logs include:

  • Medicines: name, dose, time taken, and any missed doses or side effects.
  • Blood pressure: date, time, both numbers, pulse, and notes.
  • Blood sugar: date, time, reading, whether before or after a meal, and notes about food, activity, or insulin.
  • Symptoms: dizziness, pain, sleep problems, or anything unusual.

Choosing a logbook

  • Large print and wide columns for readings and notes.
  • Clear headings so a caregiver or doctor can read it quickly.
  • Enough pages for several months.
  • A notes area on every page.
  • A pocket-friendly or desk size, depending on where it will be used.

Paper or app?

Apps can make charts and share data, but they need a phone, charging, and some confidence with technology. Paper is easier for many older adults, and a caregiver can photograph pages to share. Some families use both.

Tips for keeping it going

  1. Keep the logbook next to the monitor or pill organizer.
  2. Attach a pen with string or a clip.
  3. Record right away, not later from memory.
  4. Bring it to every appointment.
  5. Start a new book before the old one fills up.

Our own titles

The author of this site publishes large-print log books for blood pressure, blood sugar, and medicines. They are listed on the Books page. Any clear, readable logbook will do the job.

Important

A logbook is a record, not medical advice. Ask your doctor what to track, what your targets are, and what to do if numbers are out of range.

Bottom line

A simple, large-print log, filled in at the moment of each reading or dose, gives doctors better information and gives families peace of mind.
