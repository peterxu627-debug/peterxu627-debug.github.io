Choosing a Home Blood Pressure Monitor

By Peter Xu · Updated November 2026

Many doctors ask patients to check blood pressure at home, because readings in the clinic can be higher or lower than everyday numbers. A good home monitor, used correctly, gives the doctor much better information.

Upper-arm cuffs are usually preferred

Major heart-health organizations in the United States generally recommend an automatic monitor with a cuff that goes around the upper arm. Wrist monitors are smaller but are more sensitive to how the arm is held, so readings can vary more. If an upper-arm cuff is not possible (for example, a very large arm or pain), ask the doctor what to use.

Look for a validated device

"Validated" means the monitor was tested for accuracy against a standard method. In the U.S., the website validatebp.org lists devices that have been checked. It is worth looking up a model before buying.

Cuff size is critical

A cuff that is too small can give readings that are too high; one that is too large can read too low. Measure around the middle of the upper arm and compare with the cuff range on the box. Many monitors come with a "wide-range" cuff, and larger or smaller cuffs can be bought separately.

Features that help older adults

  • A large, clear display with big numbers.
  • One-button operation.
  • Memory for recent readings, and separate memory for two users if two people share it.
  • Irregular heartbeat indicator (useful information to share with the doctor, but not a diagnosis).
  • Optional phone app or Bluetooth, only if the person or caregiver will really use it.

How to measure

  1. Sit quietly for five minutes with back supported and feet flat.
  2. Rest the arm on a table at heart level.
  3. Put the cuff on bare skin, not over a sleeve.
  4. Do not talk during the reading.
  5. Take two readings a minute apart, and write both down.

Keep a log

Bring a written log or the monitor's memory to appointments. A simple paper log with the date, time, both readings, and notes (such as "missed morning pill") is easy for the doctor to read.

Important

A home monitor does not replace medical care. Follow your doctor's advice on targets and what to do with high or low readings, and call emergency services for chest pain, severe headache, or other warning signs.

Bottom line

Choose a validated upper-arm monitor with the right cuff size and a big display, use it the same way each time, and keep a log for the doctor.
